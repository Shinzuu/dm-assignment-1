Hopkins statistic

Tests whether the data has any cluster structure at all, or is just uniformly spread. He told the class to read it, and to read Example 8.9.

H = Σ xᵢᵈ / ( Σ xᵢᵈ + Σ yᵢᵈ )

TermMeaning
xᵢSample n points from the data SPACE. xᵢ is the distance from pᵢ to its nearest neighbour in D.
yᵢSample n points from D itself. yᵢ is the distance from qᵢ to its nearest neighbour in D − {qᵢ}.
dThe dimensionality of D.

Getting x and y the wrong way round inverts the answer. x starts from random points in the space; y starts from real points in the data.

If D is uniform, the two sums are close and H ≈ 0.5. If D is clustered, real points sit closer to each other than random points do, so Σxᵈ is much larger and H approaches 1.

0.72 is well above 0.5, so D has a strong clustering tendency — and indeed it has two clusters, one near 1.5 and one near 7.8.

Silhouette coefficient

He worked this on the board and said outright it must be studied for the exam. Expect it with different numbers.

a(o) = Σ dist(o, o′) / ( |Cᵢ| − 1 ) for o′ ∈ Cᵢ, o′ ≠ o

b(o) = min over Cⱼ ≠ Cᵢ of [ Σ dist(o, o′) / |Cⱼ| ]

s(o) = ( b(o) − a(o) ) / max{ a(o), b(o) }

DetailWhy it catches people
a(o) divides by |Cᵢ| − 1o is not its own neighbour. With 3 points in the cluster you divide by 2, not 3.
b(o) is a minimumAverage the distance to EACH other cluster separately, then take the smallest. With only two clusters the min is trivial, which is why the class example hid this step.
Range is −1 to 1Near 1 is compact and well separated. Negative means o is closer to another cluster than its own — a bad assignment.

0.625 is comfortably positive, so a sits well inside its own cluster. Average s(o) over a cluster to score that cluster; average over the whole data set to score the clustering.

Distance matrices

Chapter 2 material, but he told the class to revise it because hierarchical clustering is driven entirely by the distance matrix.

Euclidean: d(i,j) = √( Σ (xᵢₖ − xⱼₖ)² )

Manhattan: d(i,j) = Σ | xᵢₖ − xⱼₖ |

Minkowski: d(i,j) = ( Σ | xᵢₖ − xⱼₖ |ᵖ )^(1/p)

p = 1 gives Manhattan, p = 2 gives Euclidean. The matrix is symmetric with a zero diagonal, so only the lower triangle is ever written out.

Hierarchical clustering

Single linkage (nearest neighbour) is OUT of the syllabus — he said he could not teach it. Complete and average linkage are in.

AGNESDIANA
DirectionAgglomerative, bottom-upDivisive, top-down
StartEvery object its own clusterAll objects in one cluster
Each stepMerge the two closest clustersSplit the least cohesive cluster
StopOne cluster remainsEvery object is alone
LinkageDistance between clusters Cᵢ and Cⱼ
CompleteThe MAXIMUM distance between any point of Cᵢ and any point of Cⱼ. Gives compact, similar-sized clusters; sensitive to outliers.
AverageThe MEAN over all such pairs. A compromise between the extremes, and more robust.
CentroidThe distance between the two cluster centroids.

Neither AGNES nor DIANA can undo a merge or a split, which is why a single bad early step propagates all the way to the root.

k-means and k-medoids

k-meansk-medoids (PAM)
Centre isThe mean — a computed point that need not exist in the dataA medoid — an actual object of the data set

PAM replaces a medoid with a non-medoid when doing so lowers the total cost. The swap cost is the change in the sum of distances from every object to its nearest representative; swap when that change is negative.

Pattern mining

support(A ⇒ B) = P(A ∪ B)

confidence(A ⇒ B) = P(B | A) = support(A ∪ B) / support(A)

lift(A, B) = confidence(A ⇒ B) / support(B)

Lift above 1 means A and B occur together more than chance, so they are positively correlated. Exactly 1 means independent; below 1 means one discourages the other. Confidence alone would have called this a strong rule without telling you B is common anyway.

TermDefinition
Apriori propertyEvery non-empty subset of a frequent itemset is itself frequent. Equivalently, if an itemset is infrequent none of its supersets can be frequent — which is what lets you prune.
Closed itemsetFrequent, and no superset has the SAME support.
Maximal itemsetFrequent, and no superset is frequent at all.

Info(D) = − Σ pᵢ log₂ pᵢ

Gain(A) = Info(D) − Info_A(D)

GainRatio(A) = Gain(A) / SplitInfo(A)

Gini(D) = 1 − Σ pᵢ²

Information gain favours attributes with many distinct values; gain ratio divides that bias out with SplitInfo. Gini needs no logarithms, which is why CART uses it, and it always splits binary.

P(Cᵢ | X) ∝ P(X | Cᵢ) P(Cᵢ) = P(Cᵢ) Π P(xₖ | Cᵢ)

Naive Bayes assumes the attributes are conditionally independent given the class. If one conditional probability is zero the whole product is zero, so apply the Laplacian correction: add 1 to every count, and add the number of distinct values to each denominator.
