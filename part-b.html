Chapter 3

Data warehousing and OLAP

What a data warehouse is

Inmon's definition: “A data warehouse is a subject-oriented, integrated, time-variant, and nonvolatile collection of data in support of management’s decision-making process.” Quote it word for word.

PropertyMeaning
Subject-orientedOrganised around major subjects such as customer, product and sales, not around daily operations. Data not useful for decisions is left out.
IntegratedBuilt from several heterogeneous sources: relational databases, flat files, transaction records. Cleaning and integration make names, codes and units consistent. Example: hotel prices in different currencies, with or without tax.
Time-variantHolds history, typically 5 to 10 years. Every key structure contains an element of time, explicitly or implicitly.
NonvolatilePhysically separate from the operational data. No updates happen in place, so it needs no transaction processing, recovery or concurrency control. Only two operations: initial loading and access.

Data warehousing is the process of constructing and using data warehouses.

OLTP versus OLAP

Why keep the warehouse separate? For performance: the DBMS is tuned for OLTP (indexing, locking, logging), the warehouse for complex OLAP queries. The two also need different data. Decision support needs history, consolidation from many sources, and reconciled data quality, which operational databases do not keep.

The data cube

Dimension
A perspective the data is viewed by, such as time, item or location. Each has a dimension table, e.g. item(item_name, brand, type).
Fact table
Holds the measures, e.g. dollars_sold and units_sold, and a foreign key to each dimension table.
Measure
A numeric function evaluated at each cell. The cell {time = Q1, location = Vancouver, item = computer} holds the sum of all Q1 computer sales in Vancouver.
Cuboid
The cube at one combination of dimensions. The n-D cube at the lowest level is the base cuboid. The 0-D cuboid holding the single grand total is the apex cuboid.
Lattice
All cuboids together, from base to apex, form the data cube.

no hierarchies: number of cuboids = 2ⁿ

with hierarchies: number of cuboids = Π (Lᵢ + 1)

Lᵢ is the number of levels of dimension i, not counting the virtual top level all. If a question says the levels already include all, just multiply them, as in Problem 3.5(c).

Concept hierarchies and measures

A concept hierarchy maps low-level concepts to higher, more general ones. For location: street < city < province_or_state < country. Vancouver and Toronto roll up to Canada; New York and Chicago to USA. Rolling up and drilling down move along these hierarchies.

Trap. To roll up an average, carry sum and count and divide at the end. Averaging the averages is wrong whenever the groups differ in size.

Pattern mining: Apriori

Definitions

The slides use one running database. Every example below uses it.

TIDItems
T1{pasta, lemon, bread, orange}
T2{pasta, lemon}
T3{pasta, orange, cake}
T4{pasta, lemon, orange, cake}
Item set I
All items sold: {pasta, lemon, bread, orange, cake}. It has 2⁵ − 1 = 31 non-empty subsets.
Transaction
A set of items with a unique TID. Unordered; each item appears at most once.
k-itemset
An itemset of k items. {pasta, lemon} is a 2-itemset.
Support
sup(X) = number of transactions containing X. sup({pasta, orange}) = 3, or 75% as a ratio.
Frequent itemset
An itemset with support ≥ minsup. Frequent itemset mining lists all of them.

A higher minsup gives fewer frequent itemsets, and mining becomes faster and needs less memory.

support(A ⇒ B) = P(A ∪ B) = sup(A ∪ B) / |D|

confidence(A ⇒ B) = P(B | A) = sup(A ∪ B) / sup(A)

lift(A, B) = confidence(A ⇒ B) / support(B)

A rule is strong when it meets both min_sup and min_conf. Lift above 1 means A and B are positively correlated; 1 means independent; below 1 means negatively correlated.

The Apriori property

Property 1 (anti-monotone): if X ⊂ Y then sup(Y) ≤ sup(X). Example: sup({pasta}) = 4, sup({pasta, lemon}) = 3, sup({pasta, lemon, orange}) = 2.

Property 2: if any subset of Y is infrequent, Y is infrequent. If {bread} is infrequent, {bread, lemon} and every other superset of bread can be skipped without counting.

This is what shrinks the search space. The naive approach counts all 2ⁿ − 1 itemsets, reading every transaction for each. On the slide example the naive approach considers 31 itemsets; Apriori considers 18.

The Apriori algorithm

  1. Scan the database to count every 1-itemset (C₁). Keep those with support ≥ minsup (L₁).
  2. Join: combine pairs of frequent (k−1)-itemsets into candidate k-itemsets (Cₖ).
  3. Prune: delete any candidate with an infrequent (k−1)-subset (Property 2).
  4. Scan: count the remaining candidates in the database. Keep those ≥ minsup (Lₖ).
  5. Repeat from step 2 until no candidates can be generated.

Steps 3 to 6: the six pairs come from joining the four frequent items. None has an infrequent subset, so none is pruned before counting. {lemon, cake} fails minsup.

Problems of Apriori: it can generate a huge number of candidates; it scans the database once per level; and candidates may not exist in the database at all.

Generating association rules

For each frequent itemset l and each non-empty proper subset s, output s ⇒ (l − s) if sup(l) / sup(s) ≥ min_conf. Support is already known, so no further scan is needed.

FP-growth in brief

  1. Scan 1: count items, drop the infrequent ones, sort the rest by falling support.
  2. Scan 2: insert each transaction's sorted frequent items as a path in the FP-tree, sharing common prefixes and adding to node counts. A header table links every node of each item.
  3. Mine each item from the bottom of the header table: collect its prefix paths (the conditional pattern base), build a conditional FP-tree from them, and grow patterns recursively.

FP-growth scans the database twice and generates no candidates. Problem 4.6(a) in Part A works the full tree and compares it with Apriori.

Chapter 6

Classification

Basic concepts

Supervised (classification)Unsupervised (clustering)
Training dataEach tuple has a class labelNo class labels
GoalLearn a model that labels new dataDiscover classes or clusters in the data

Classification predicts categorical class labels. Numeric prediction models continuous-valued functions. Applications: credit approval, medical diagnosis, fraud detection, web page categorisation.

Two-step process. (1) Model construction: learn rules, a decision tree or a formula from the training set. (2) Model usage: estimate accuracy on an independent test set, the percentage of test tuples classified correctly. If it is acceptable, classify new tuples. Testing on the training data gives an over-optimistic estimate (overfitting).

Decision tree induction

A decision tree is a flowchart-like tree: each internal node tests an attribute, each branch is an outcome of the test, and each leaf holds a class label. The top node is the root.

  1. Start with all training tuples at the root. Attributes are categorical; continuous ones are discretised first.
  2. Pick the test attribute by a selection measure: information gain, gain ratio or Gini index.
  3. Partition the tuples by the attribute's values and recurse on each partition (top-down, greedy, divide and conquer).
  4. Stop when all tuples at a node have one class, or no attributes remain (label the leaf by majority vote), or no tuples remain.

Info(D) = − Σ pᵢ log₂ pᵢ

Info_A(D) = Σ (|Dⱼ| / |D|) × Info(Dⱼ)

Gain(A) = Info(D) − Info_A(D)

SplitInfo_A(D) = − Σ (|Dⱼ| / |D|) log₂ (|Dⱼ| / |D|)

GainRatio(A) = Gain(A) / SplitInfo_A(D)

Gini(D) = 1 − Σ pᵢ²

Gini_A(D) = (|D₁|/|D|) Gini(D₁) + (|D₂|/|D|) Gini(D₂)

Naive Bayesian classification

Bayesian classifiers are statistical: they predict the probability that a tuple belongs to each class. They are fast and accurate on large databases.

P(H | X) = P(X | H) P(H) / P(X) posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence

P(X) is the same for every class, so only P(X | Cᵢ) P(Cᵢ) needs maximising. The naive step is class-conditional independence: attributes are assumed independent given the class.

P(X | Cᵢ) = Π P(xₖ | Cᵢ) = P(x₁|Cᵢ) × P(x₂|Cᵢ) × … × P(xₙ|Cᵢ)

For a categorical attribute, P(xₖ | Cᵢ) = tuples of Cᵢ with value xₖ, divided by the number of tuples of Cᵢ. For a continuous attribute, use a Gaussian with that class's mean μ and standard deviation σ.

Trap. If one conditional probability is 0, the whole product is 0. Use the Laplacian correction: add 1 to every count and add the number of distinct values to the denominator. Problem 6.7(c) in Part A shows the case.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Easy to implement; good results in most cases.Class-conditional independence rarely holds. Dependencies such as symptoms and disease cannot be modelled. Bayesian belief networks handle them.

k-nearest neighbour, eager and lazy

kNN stores the training tuples. To classify X, find the k closest tuples by a distance such as Euclidean, and take the majority class. Normalise attributes first, or the one with the widest range dominates the distance.

Chapter 8

Cluster analysis

Basic concepts

A cluster is a collection of objects similar to one another and dissimilar to objects in other clusters. Clustering is unsupervised: no predefined classes. It is used on its own to see the data distribution, or as preprocessing for summarisation, compression (vector quantisation), finding nearest neighbours, and outlier detection.

A good clustering has high intra-class similarity (cohesive) and low inter-class similarity (distinct). Quality depends on the similarity measure, the implementation, and the ability to find hidden patterns.

ApproachIdeaMethods
PartitioningBuild k partitions and improve them by a criterion such as sum of squared error.k-means, k-medoids, CLARANS
HierarchicalBuild a hierarchical decomposition, bottom-up or top-down.AGNES, DIANA, BIRCH, Chameleon
Density-basedGrow clusters while the neighbourhood density is high enough.DBSCAN, OPTICS, DENCLUE
Grid-basedQuantise the space into a grid of cells and cluster the cells.STING, WaveCluster, CLIQUE

Distance measures and the distance matrix

Euclidean: d(i, j) = √( Σ (xᵢ − yᵢ)² )

Manhattan: d(i, j) = Σ | xᵢ − yᵢ |

Minkowski: d(i, j) = ( Σ | xᵢ − yᵢ |ᵖ )^(1/p) p = 1 Manhattan, p = 2 Euclidean

Named for the exam. He asked the class to revise the distance matrix from Chapter 2: hierarchical clustering runs entirely on it. It is an n × n table of d(i, j), symmetric with a zero diagonal, so write only the lower triangle.

k-means

  1. Place k points in the space as the initial centroids.
  2. Assign each object to the cluster with the closest centroid.
  3. When all objects are assigned, recompute each centroid as the mean of its cluster.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the stopping criterion holds.

Stopping criteria: no object changes cluster, or the squared error falls below a small threshold.

SE = Σᵢ Σ_{p ∈ Cᵢ} | p − mᵢ |² mᵢ = mean of cluster Cᵢ

k-means: prosk-means: cons
Guaranteed to converge, to a local optimum (not necessarily the global one). Low cost: O(nkt) for n objects, k clusters, t iterations.k must be specified. Sensitive to noise and outliers, which pull the mean. Sensitive to the initial centroids, so results can differ between runs. Needs a mean, so numeric data.

Evaluation of clustering

Three tasks: assess clustering tendency (is there non-random structure at all?), determine the number of clusters, and measure clustering quality.

Hopkins statistic

Named for the exam. Named in class, together with Example 8.9.

  1. Sample n points p₁ … pₙ uniformly from the data space. xᵢ = distance from pᵢ to its nearest neighbour in D.
  2. Sample n points q₁ … qₙ uniformly from D without replacement. yᵢ = distance from qᵢ to its nearest neighbour in D − {qᵢ}.
  3. Compute H. d is the dimensionality of D.

H = Σ xᵢᵈ / ( Σ xᵢᵈ + Σ yᵢᵈ )

If D is uniform, the two sums are close and H ≈ 0.5. If D is clustered, real points lie closer to their neighbours than random points do, so Σxᵈ is much larger and H approaches 1.

0.72 is well above 0.5 and close to 1, so D has a strong clustering tendency. It has two clusters, around 1.5 and around 7.8.

Trap. x comes from random points in the space, y from real points in D. Swap them and H inverts. Each nearest neighbour is searched in D; for qᵢ, exclude qᵢ itself.

Silhouette coefficient

Named for the exam. He worked this on the board and said it must be read for the exam. Expect it with new numbers.

a(o) = Σ dist(o, o′) / (|Cᵢ| − 1) o′ ∈ Cᵢ, o′ ≠ o

b(o) = min over Cⱼ ≠ Cᵢ of Σ dist(o, o′) / |Cⱼ|

s(o) = (b(o) − a(o)) / max{a(o), b(o)}

a(o)
Average distance from o to the other objects of its own cluster. Small means compact.
b(o)
Average distance from o to the objects of each other cluster; take the smallest. Large means well separated.
s(o)
Between −1 and 1. Near 1: o is well placed. Near 0: o is on a border. Negative: o is closer to another cluster than its own.

0.667 is well above 0.5 and close to 1, so P1 sits well inside cluster A and far from cluster B.

Average s(o) over a cluster to judge that cluster, and over all objects to judge the whole clustering.

Trap. Divide a(o) by |Cᵢ| − 1, because o is not compared with itself. Take b(o) as the minimum of the per-cluster averages, never the average over all other points.
