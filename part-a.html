Warehouse of three dimensions time, doctor and

(a) Three schema classes used to model data

warehouses:

1. Star schema — one fact table, each

dimension a single flat table.

2. Snowflake schema — dimensions are

normalised into sub-tables.

3. Fact constellation — several fact

and three dimensions:

Problem 8.17

Describe k-means, k-medoids, BIRCH and DBSCAN

in terms of the shapes of cluster they find,

the input parameters they need, and their

limitations.

(a) k-means

Shapes: spherical and convex, of

roughly equal size.

Input: k, the number of clusters. Limits:

k must be known in advance;

the result depends on the initial centres and

may be a local optimum; the mean

is not robust, so outliers drag centres; needs

numeric data; cannot find non-convex shapes.

(b) k-medoids, as in PAM

Shapes: spherical and convex, as

with k-means.

Input: k. Limits: far more robust to outliers,

because a medoid is an actual object and

it minimises absolute error, not squared error;

Problem 8.1716 / 17

but each iteration costs O(k(n-k)^2), so it does

not scale; k is still needed; still poor

on non-convex shapes.

Problem 8.17 continued

(c) BIRCH

Shapes: spherical, because a cluster

is summarised by a CF triple and judged

by its radius and diameter.

Input: branching factor B and

threshold T for the CF tree, plus available

memory, and k if the optional global clustering

runs.

Limits: numeric data only; the result

depends on the order the data arrives in;

sensitive to T; poor on non-spherical shapes. In

exchange it needs one scan and is incremental.

(d) DBSCAN

Shapes: arbitrary. Any density

connected shape, convex or not, and it labels

outliers as noise.

Input: Eps, the radius, and MinPts,

the least neighbours for a core point. Note

that k is not required.

Limits: one global Eps and MinPts

cannot suit clusters of widely different

density; the result is sensitive to both;

distances lose meaning in high dimensions; cost

is O(n^2) without a spatial index, and

O(n log n) with one.
